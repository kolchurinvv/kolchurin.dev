## Through-lines

Recurring patterns I want the story to mark honestly each time they surface. Tagged inline as `*[Pattern → name]*` so a future pass can pull a list of moments per pattern, and so a curated public version can choose how — or whether — to surface them.

- **self-sabotage** — moments where I made a choice that I knew, even at the time, wasn't going to take me in the direction I wanted. Still present in me today. The story keeps this candid rather than smoothing it over.
- **underlying-logic** — a strong pull toward subjects and problems that have a logical structure I can derive, instead of facts I have to memorise from a book verbatim. Math, Pascal, operations management (the formula-shaped part of business admin), later: declarative systems and DevOps. Whenever I find the underlying idea, I can run with it; when there isn't one, I bounce off.
- **english-as-substrate** — a deliberate, conscious decision to align my life with the language the modern coding syntax is built out of. Python, JavaScript, most of what we use day-to-day takes its verbs from English. Moving to New York was partly that choice: the hobby that ran in the background for years should sit in the same language as the people who invented coding as we know it. Forcing my brain to rewire toward English wasn't a side-effect of the move; it was one of the reasons for it.

The character was already there before the technology was. Pre-1997 — before my father brought home the family computer that opens Chapter 1 — the Vovych on record is a four-year-old in Novosibirsk who:

> *Vovych meets the machine.* His father carries a tower into the house. It has 16 MB of RAM, which is respectable for the neighbourhood; somebody down the street has 32, which is absurd. Vovych is five. He floors the gas pedal in Need for Speed and drives directly into the first wall, repeatedly, with conviction.

My first memory of a computer is my father bringing one home. I think it was 1997, so I was around five. It was a pretty good "modern office" desktop by the standards of the time — already a tower, sixteen megs of RAM. That was kind of powerful in my circle. There were people who had thirty-two megs and it was *crazy* big for us back then.

The thing I remember playing on it was a very early build of Need for Speed. I couldn't get past the first couple of corners most of the time, because I was pressing the gas constantly and just didn't care whether I crashed into a wall or not. That's pretty much how I remember it.

The first time I actually *did* something on a computer was probably around ten. I was downloading Winamp and downloading ICQ and changing skins on both of them, swapping styles and themes around. I changed the background on Windows 95. I poked through the system configuration screens just to see what was in there — I don't really remember what I was looking at, but I remember opening them. We had a printer, so I'd print stuff for the sake of printing it.

Most of those memories are already on Windows XP though — that's when it picked up. More ICQ skins, more background changes, installing games (without modifying them yet). I don't honestly remember why we had a computer in the first place. It took me a while to get my head around what to do with the thing. I think my parents needed it for work — that's why we had the printer too.

That machine stayed in the living room. It was definitely the family's computer. We even had a proper computer desk with CD slots on both sides for the discs. Each of us had our own username and profile — there were three of us, me, my mom, my dad — and we used it together. After school I'd try to play games instead of doing my homework, and somehow my father always knew. I never admitted to it, but he'd always either suspect or just *know*. I don't know what he did — maybe there was some logging mechanism he'd installed. Every time I touched the computer I'd try to put everything back in its place: keyboard, mouse, everything lined up the way it was. He still always knew somebody had played on it. And there was only one suspect. He was strict about too much time on video games.

The first computer I actually got for *myself* wasn't until high school. I came back to Russia from a year of study abroad in Alabama (2006) and was finishing up ninth grade, and that's when I got my first laptop. I can't remember the make and model for the life of me. It was fairly underpowered, but it was good enough for me.

The high school I finished at was Lyceum No. 22 "Hope of Siberia" (*Лицей № 22 «Надежда Сибири»*). Somehow I got roped into being the sound engineer for the entire school — every performance, every recital, basically everything parent-facing that the school put on to occupy the kids, I was on the sound for. The physics teacher helped me out at first, but once he figured I was good enough to handle it on my own, he stepped back. I got some software to help me — a pirated Adobe something, the one for editing raw sound files. That was the first time my own computer was actually doing real work.

There was a moment somebody pointed at me, but it happened when I wasn't in the room. The lyceum had just absorbed a wave of newcomers for tenth and eleventh grade — the existing students and the new arrivals got reshuffled between four concurrent classes — and one of the teachers needed some audio work done for a presentation. They wanted to know who could run the mixer. Somebody said *yeah, he can do it.* I wasn't even there. I was told afterwards that this had happened.

So I just ran with it, and I was pretty good at it. There was another kid who'd been volunteered the same way — he didn't sign up either, people just pointed at him — and I had to have a conversation with him about it, because I really *wanted* to do this. It was very different from my previous high school experience and it just seemed like a lot of fun.

In Audition I just started poking at all the buttons. I tried to record audio, tried to remix songs, tried to put audio snippets together. That was the whole method. I mostly wanted to stitch sound effects together for sketches and presentations, and for that I just searched online for whatever I needed.

The physics teacher showed me the hardware side — how the speaker connects to the amplifier, the amplifier to the mixer, what the different effects on the board did, how to hook up the wireless microphones. Beyond that I was dropped into the deep end, and I wanted to explore it because it was a lot of fun. It also opened up a lot of new connections for me all over the school. There was a KVN[^1] team at the lyceum that needed sound effects and snippets for their sketches, and that pulled me in further.

[^1]: KVN (КВН — *Клуб Весёлых и Находчивых*) — long-running Soviet/post-Soviet comedy improv league, in school/student form basically a sketch-comedy team. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KVN).

### Code, sideways

Somewhere around that time I had a summer class on website design. It was so, so bad — only two weeks, pure frame-based HTML, horrendously structured. The teacher couldn't figure out how to make a menu in one frame refresh the main "hero" frame; the links kept breaking, and I think they were consulting a book in real time. But it did show me one thing: you can do something with a computer that's *immediately consumable*. You see the result of your work right away. That was amazing.

At the same time, our lyceum had an informatics class, and we did Turbo Pascal. It was bad, but what it was, was English-language structured in a way that eliminates ambiguity — it translates your intentions into a deterministic pattern. There's that old adage: *the best thing about computers is that they do what you tell them to; the worst thing about computers is that they do what you tell them to.* That's exactly it. You have to structure your thoughts in a way that won't fail. Which was great, because I really loved math. I skipped a lot of school, but math was one of the things I still kind of enjoyed even in high school — I still watch science-educational videos about pure math, even though I'm nowhere near a mathematician. Turbo Pascal scratched the same itch.

We even put a team together and went to compete at the national level. We swept the regional Olympiad — beat everybody locally — and then went on to the nationals. We didn't place. We didn't really achieve anything. But going was the thing. I think it was the end of grade ten or the start of grade eleven; I don't remember exactly. One problem stuck with me: given the dimensions of an airplane cabin, figure out the matrix of seating arrangements — aisles, aisle widths, minimum and maximum legroom per row. That kind of problem just lodged itself in my head.

The rest of grade eleven was mostly prep for the new mandatory standardized exams. We were one of the first cohorts that had to take them to be accepted into universities, so they ate the year. We didn't do much programming after the Olympiad.

What I *was* doing on my own around then was JavaScript and frontend. I remember going to sleep trying to figure out the best way to write a calendar — leap years, all of it — turning the algorithm over in my head until I drifted off. That was already personal exploration, not school.

But it was still a hobby at that point. I wasn't thinking of it as a career. What was on the horizon after high school was moving to the Czech Republic. My parents and I had been discussing it a lot, and the summer before graduation we went together to scout universities. We settled on the University of Economics in Prague. I was going to go into business administration. I was studying Czech back in Russia and prepping for the exams. That's pretty much where the developer side of me sat during those years — in the background.

### Where the JS actually came from

Honestly, I don't remember where my JavaScript / frontend learning *continued* from. It was always a hobby. I'd just find myself in a place where I could probably do a mock website for somebody.

The thing I actually *did* during that stretch was a summer job for one of my mom's companies. A different company had originally written their sites, and they needed maintenance — new submission forms, content updates, minor changes. This was back when everything was on prem; I remember my father and a couple of other IT guys setting up servers in the back room of the office. My mom ran a tourist agency, and there was also a kind of convention they organised for creatives to show off on one stage with juries and everything. Those were the two sites I helped maintain.

We didn't really have a proper admin dashboard. Or — there was one, it's just that sometimes it was easier to just go into the source code and change stuff in the source code. Because I was so green I didn't know about linters and LSPs (I don't even know if LSPs were a thing back then). So it was a lot of manual checking and rechecking and making sure everything still ran. Every little comma or semicolon could break the whole thing. Tedious manual work.

But I *did* something during that summer job. I think that's where the JS thread quietly kept going — because at some point I needed to justify to my parents that all the time I was spending on the computer wasn't just video games. I needed a proper answer that I was actually *learning* something. I don't even know if it worked on them. But it has paid off for me now.

> The sites themselves didn't survive — we didn't know about git or any version control at the time, and they got lost through later migrations. There were also a couple of one-offs I don't have anymore: helping a friend from a different school finish an IT-class task, and just messing around on his home PC for fun.

### Loose ends from those years

A few small but honest answers worth keeping on the record:

- **Piracy / Linux phase.** I never installed Linux back then. But pirated games with crack keygens — everyone in Russia had those. I never tried making one; I just enjoyed cracked games. :)
- **First time something actually *worked* after hours of frustration.** The websites I was helping people with. How we figured out anything is a complete blank to me now — but the moment the thing finally rendered the right way, that was the feeling.
- **When I became "the computer person."** High school. The moment the sound-guy tag got attached to me, I became the tech-guy at the same moment.

### Prague

After graduating eleventh grade in Russia in May/June 2009, I made my first long-term move to Prague — first step on Czech soil that wasn't just a visit was 9 September 2009. (I'd been to the country twice before, about a week at a time.) I spent nine months at a Czech-language prep course at UniPrep, then during the next enrolment season passed the entrance exams into the University of Economics in Prague (VŠE), business development major.

I dropped out after one school year. I enrolled at ČVUT — the Czech Technical University in Prague — and dropped out of that too. By 2014 I was in a pretty bad place. I got really depressed. I sold my car in Prague, bought a one-way ticket to New York, and on **27 December 2014** I left.

Two Prague-era sites still exist in my projects folder: `amiga.cz` (around 2012–2013) and `poligon.cz` (around the beginning of 2014). That's roughly the visible footprint of what I was doing on the side during the university years — frontend work for whoever needed it.

### What broke

It was a diffused slide into limbo. I didn't know what to do, didn't know what my life was going to be like.

Business administration as a *whole field* wasn't for me. The parts I actually liked were the ones with logical structure — operations management with its formulas, the bits that worked the way math works. Same through-line as Pascal in school: pick up the underlying idea and run with it, instead of memorising the book verbatim for the test. The rest I had no interest in.

The next university I enrolled at — ČVUT — I picked in panic. I needed paperwork to stay in the country, and ironically it was the easiest one to get into; the faculty I walked into had something to do with nuclear physics, and it kept its enrolment dates very late. There was a sub-major within it that was supposed to be IT-flavoured. But sitting there it felt like four-to-one high-level math against any actual programming, and the programming was done in Delphi — which, with my limited knowledge of the industry at the time, already felt to me like a dying language.

The language barrier didn't help. I could write papers back then (I can't anymore). Tests were tough — new vocabulary, new terms — but manageable. The bigger problem was day-to-day. I never managed to connect with Czech students or properly integrate into the social structure of the country. I fell into the easier trap of being friends only with people I'd met in the language course, and all of them were Russian-speakers — from all over the former Soviet Union, but the point remains. Casual conversations in Czech were a big hurdle then and still are. I'm back in Prague now and integration is still a real problem for me.

So: wrong field, then wrong school for the wrong reason, no local friends, language holding day-to-day life at arm's length. The slide.

I don't think the schools were badly taught or bureaucratic — it wasn't them. The blame is on me, given the previous factors. I don't want to get into excuses. This is just what happened.

### Family

Somewhere during those years my father died.

He had epilepsy. Around that time Russia had stopped importing the specific medication that managed his seizures, and I had to basically smuggle it in. I found a doctor who'd write me prescriptions, and then I'd go to the airport, find people flying into Russia, hand them a few boxes — fully packaged, with the prescriptions — and ask them to trust me that it wasn't drugs.

After he died, my mother and I had a really rough patch. He died on the heels of their tourist agency going bankrupt, and — this being Russia — there was some racketeering involved. We lost the apartment. We lost the office space. We were really, really broke. My mom eventually bounced back in a new industry.

### Patching it back together

Then there was a good patch where I started helping my mom directly. I was translating documents for her, English to Russian and back. I'm not a lawyer, but my English was solid, and I found a template library of different contracts I could lean on for the right verbiage. I also went with her to a few countries as a live interpreter for deals — they were trying to open up accounts in Singapore, in Hong Kong, in Dubai.

That was the stretch where I actually managed to scrape together some savings. By the time I was going to New York I had a few thousand dollars in my account — enough to last me a year, maybe.

### Why New York

My only previous experience of the US had been the study-abroad year in Alabama back in 2006 — and it had left me with a weird, almost certain notion that I'd never come back. I only realised later that what I actually disliked wasn't the US; it was specifically the *South*.

When I was debating where to go next from Czechia, I knew I wanted to keep speaking English. The UK didn't seem like a good choice — I didn't know anyone there. Australia, New Zealand, South Africa: same reason. That left the US.

My aunt's family lived there — her husband and two children, both a bit older than me. They had hosted me in Alabama back in 2006, and it had originally been my aunt's idea to send me to study there. Huge thanks to her. A *lot* of my life since has depended on me having really good English.

So I went to the US — but not to where any of my relatives lived. That way I wouldn't seem like I was trying to leech off them, while still having them somewhere in the country as a safety net in case of emergencies. New York fit.

There was a deeper reason underneath all of that, though, and I knew it at the time. *[Pattern → english-as-substrate]* Python, JavaScript, basically everything I'd ever poked at in code took its verbs from English. English is the substrate the modern syntax is built out of. The hobby I'd been carrying in the background for years deserved to sit inside the same language as the people who'd invented it. I was already decent in English — going to a place where my day-to-day would force my brain to rewire toward it was a way of aligning my whole life with the language coding actually speaks.

### Landing — Madison Square Garden, then Williamsburg

The first four months I lived in this overcrowded apartment I'd found on Craigslist or something like that — right next to Madison Square Garden. I don't remember the exact street, but you could see the Garden from our rooftop, a block over. It was really loud. The apartment had a living room, then a small room, then a bigger room — each of the bigger rooms had its own bathroom, and one shared kitchen. All of that was wall-to-wall covered with double-decker beds. At max capacity it could "house" around eighteen people. It was horrible and super illegal by New York rules, and we got shut down after four months of me being there.

I found something similar in Williamsburg, right off the Williamsburg Bridge, first stop on the L — 185 South 4th Street. The bottom line was that instead of eighteen people crammed into a very small apartment, it was eighteen people in a very, *very* large one. Three-story-ish penthouse with another apartment above us. We had a lot of rooms across the three levels. One of the rooms still had double-decker beds, but because of the square footage of the place it wasn't illegal. So that was great. I moved into one of those bunks again for a few years. I stayed in that apartment maybe three or four years total — out of around seven years in NYC overall, most of it was there.

The apartment itself was *interesting*. The owner had a deal with a modeling agency, so male models would actually live there — a revolving door, basically. The bigger rooms were for longer-term tenants. That detail matters later.

### Drift — first ~year, no real income

For the first couple of months, basically the whole first year, I wasn't really working. I was living off the savings. I leaned on my mom for some help. I'd still do an occasional translation for her, but that thread wasn't really working anymore. I tried working at a gay nightclub — Koechek — just to have some pocket money to walk around with.

For maybe the first two or three years I didn't really *work*. I was trying to figure out what to do. I applied to college over there — CUNY, SUNY — they didn't accept me. I made a friend from Brazil and went to visit him in São Paulo for a month. That's actually when I was trying to work on the `VladaShamova` site (summer 2015).

### Construction — Sheepshead Bay

Somewhere during my Williamsburg years our financial situation — my mom and I — took another downward turn. No more savings. I needed to find a job, and through some friends I found a gig at Sheepshead Bay, near Brighton Beach. Forty-five-minute subway ride every morning.

It was a good company of people, though. Best general contractors I've ever seen, and I've seen a few. The neatest, cleanest, actually did all the things they said they would for the clients. I really appreciated that — it was a lot easier to work with them than with any other crew. With my eye for precision and neatness, I ended up doing a lot of tiling and a lot of cutting of those tiles. We got commissioned to work on other projects in Manhattan, in high-value apartments overlooking Central Park. About six months in total. The work-ethic thing stuck with me from that crew.

### Cometa Group — the test assignment

During the construction stretch, through my housemate Bobby in the Williamsburg place, I got introduced to **Mike** — Bobby's former boss. Mike's company, **Cometa Group**, gave me a test assignment.

### The SoccerPulse rewrite

SoccerPulse had a really bad maintenance pattern. Nobody had invested time into upgrading anything underneath — Vue, Vuetify, Node, all of it was so out of date that any new feature, even a new table, became practically impossible to retrofit into the existing site.

### Building the machine — April 2025

I'd wanted to build a gaming PC since the NYC era. Felt like five years of wanting and never quite having the money to go all in at once. ### Choosing the OS — Nobara, NixOS, and the agent-built way out

I considered paying about $200 for a Windows Pro license. I'd also been watching gaming-on-Linux and work-on-Linux YouTubers for five or six years at that point, and I thought: *let's give it a whirl*.

I started with **Nobara** for gaming and **NixOS** for work, dual-booted. Nobara is good for gaming — kernel fixes specific to NVIDIA — and NixOS could be the stable, predictable, declarative side.

NixOS was hard. The syntax is confusing and I'm still not fully fluent in it even a year and a half of daily-driving later. Flutter on Fedora (which is Nobara's base) is hard to install too, just less hard than on NixOS. Around this time the newly-rebranded Cometa — now **Sticky Ventures** (the September 2024 rebrand was a *real* role pivot, not just a relabel) — paid for my Flutter course and told me, in so many words: *the old SoccerPulse website is being retired, reposition yourself to Flutter or you won't have a job.* Not stated out loud, but plainly implied.

So I dove into Flutter. I did the course on Fedora, in VSCode. It was fine — nothing to write home about — except I hated that everything in Flutter is a class. By that point I'd already drifted toward seeing functional programming as the right call in many cases, and *everything-must-be-a-class* broke my mind a little.

While that was running, my "always pull the latest update the moment it lands" instinct kept breaking Nobara. On the day a Fedora update rolls out, things are generally broken — stutters, black screens, all of it. I broke it twice that way.

Then I tried to *rewire GRUB2* to launch the systemd-boot for NixOS — I wanted one launcher to switch between Bazzite/Nobara and NixOS without leaning on the BIOS. The actual right answer is much simpler: the EFI boot binary that lets you override the *next-boot* variable, then a `sudo reboot` flips you to the other system. I didn't know that yet. Trying to rewire GRUB broke my video drivers; stuttery image, nothing really worked, I had to nuke the machine again.

That was the breaking point. There's no way I can reliably work if I'm rebuilding the machine every other weekend. One Friday evening I sat down and over the course of about two and a half days bit the bullet and figured out how to get Flutter running on NixOS via flakes.

That weekend is when the bulk of the [PlayerPulse flake](/home/_liminor/Dev_Projects/Build_Sticky/PlayerPulse_org/playerpulse/flake.nix) was born. Now I use flakes as per-project dev shells so binaries from different projects don't collide — none of them argue about which environment to use; they're neatly separated; stale environments come up only occasionally when I bump deps inside a project.

### The year-long NixOS + NVIDIA + emulator tail

The flake's *skeleton* landed that weekend, but it took almost a year to actually figure out the full dependency story — specifically how to pass NVIDIA drivers in so the Android emulator would pick them up, and how to set the flake up so qemu-based emulation had hardware acceleration. Without that, everything was on the CPU. Sometimes the emulator ran relatively well for a while, but it would still stutter and drop frames.

Then I also learned that GNOME's **Mutter** is really bad at frame pacing for apps that run on X11 — and the emulator's bundled Qt isn't compatible with Wayland, so it falls back to X11 by force, which routes it straight through Mutter's bad pacing. Two unrelated problems combining into the worst possible UX.

That whole investigation is what eventually became the flake — see `docs/flutter-nixos-gist.md` for the planned generalised write-up. The short version is: there's almost no proper documentation online for this combination (modern NVIDIA + Android emulator with Vulkan + NixOS-impure GPU driver bridge + Mutter-vs-X11), and what exists is buried deep in GitHub.

### Bazzite replaces Nobara

Nobara had to go — I went on the hunt for a stable gaming distro. Last attempt. If it didn't work, I'd swallow the Windows tithe.

What I found was **Bazzite** — and pleasantly, it turned out to be a *declarative* fork of Fedora Silverblue. Which meant I now had two declarative distros on the same machine: one for gaming, one for working. After installing Bazzite and liking the performance on the gaming side, the same weekend was the weekend the NixOS side actually came together. Both halves clicked. **Deno** in particular runs really easily on NixOS, so the backend half of my job bridged over without fuss.

### Out of GNOME → Niri + DMS

Niri was actually a left turn. I'd been eyeing **Hyprland** first.

One day I tried to install Hyprland and it didn't work with my NVIDIA drivers. While poking around for alternatives I accidentally saw that **Niri** was another contender for a good Wayland compositor. At that point I'd already started paying for ChatGPT 5.3 Codex and had installed the PI agent. With the agent driving, I basically rewrote the entire Hyprland branch of my NixOS config to use Niri instead, launched it, and the drivers picked up immediately.

The keyboard-everywhere stack rounds out like this:

### Self-hosting philosophy

I like doing things myself. I don't see a big advantage of going the enterprise route when you don't have the user base, the traffic, or the genuine need for proper enterprise stuff like Kubernetes. It has its place, but most of us aren't there. Even at my current job — thousands of users — a lot of the K8s scaffolding is nonsensical for the size we are; we're overpaying for infrastructure.

I don't see the point of Slack when **Rocket.Chat** and **Mattermost** exist and you can run them on a $15/month VPS — rather than paying thousands of dollars a year to a company that trains its own internal models on your chat history, doesn't let you own it, and can hold it hostage against you if you stop paying. That's a shitty business model. Whatever Salesforce is doing with that, I'm not a big fan of.

### Dokploy — outgrowing, not rejecting

Worth being precise about this: **Dokploy isn't an attempt-plus-rejection** the way the public sketch of me sometimes makes it sound. Dokploy is actually what's hosting the current first-draft level of `kolchurin.dev` right now, and I *like* it.

What I can't swallow is the pricing math. The cloud version is ~$5/server, plus $5/month for the VPS itself, so already $10 for one deployment. Want another deployment with a different stack? Another $5 for Dokploy plus $5 for the second VPS. That's $20 for two services where the underlying VPS cost would be $10 — you're doubling your VPS bill per service.

So I'm planning to retire Dokploy. I'd rather pay $5 *one time* to an agent like Claude to set up systemd with the same exact feature set — zero-downtime rolling deploys, rollbacks, webhook triggers, all of it — and then that pattern works for me on every future VPS for free. **Watchtower** is another option, though it's effectively deprecated and doesn't support Podman, and I want to go Podman.

### The local AI lab

The LiteLLM proxy + Open-WebUI setup is something I saw NetworkChuck do on his YouTube channel, and I thought it would be a cool weekend project to have one place where I can put all of my API keys — Anthropic, OpenAI, Google — and just have a model available to me anywhere.

The same VPS that's been running my **Pi-hole** DNS server for years was the obvious host; I just scaled the instance up to house the new services alongside it. **WireGuard** was already terminating on that VPS. From there it was a natural extension: route Ollama requests from Open-WebUI back through the tunnel to my workstation. It worked. So when I need a dumb question that doesn't justify a massive context window, I can spin up a locally-run model and it answers me wherever I am, as long as my workstation is up. I installed a PWA via Firefox on iOS that logs straight into Open-WebUI.

The LiteLLM layer gives me **temporary sudo-style keys** and **usage limits**. So in addition to org-level usage limits on the upstream providers (OpenAI, Google, Anthropic), there's another layer of limits at LiteLLM. Multiple layers of safety: even if a key gets stolen, the blast radius is bounded.

I've also wired **Neovim** to use the LiteLLM-proxied local models. The reason proxying a local model through LiteLLM into Open-WebUI is worth doing is that Open-WebUI **persists conversations to disk on the VPS**, so I have a searchable history that outlasts any individual model's tiny context window. The model forgets; the database doesn't.

The current experiment is **llama.cpp**, because Ollama leaves real performance on the table when you're trying to do something more demanding. I want my own ranking and embedding system using local models. The context window is the killer: Gemma 4 31B with a 49k-token context already occupies somewhere around 15 GB of system RAM *plus* all 15 GB of my VRAM. The weights themselves fit on the GPU — the context blows past it.

So the next step is something lower-level, where I can fiddle with **quantization** and other parameters I'm not really familiar with yet. *Temperature* and *top-p* don't cut it anymore.

**Where I am now:**

Almost everything I do, I do from a keyboard inside a terminal. The desktop is niri with DankMaterialShell — I left GNOME specifically because I wanted keyboard-first to mean *everywhere*, not just in Vim. The browser is Zen with Vimium. Modifiers are scrambled at the hardware level via keyd so the same physical key means the same thing regardless of the OS underneath. tmux is the session manager; Neovim is the editor; Nushell is the shell; Ghostty is the terminal. They all agree, more or less, that motion is `hjkl`, that escape is `jj`, that the mouse is optional.

I work in TS and Go and Python and a bit of Rust now. The Rust shows up when I need a small daemon that has to be reliable — like the notification-to-window-focus helper I wrote to bridge DMS notifications and niri. Small sharp tools beat big general ones; that's the pattern under everything.

I like OSS. I read source. I prefer projects I can fork over services I can only rent. When I host something I rent the smallest VPS I can get away with and run static sites behind systemd units, with Watchtower or webhooks handling rolling updates. I tried Dokploy and bounced — the overhead wasn't earning its keep. The same minimalism shows up in my dotfiles: a selectively-tracked `~/.config` repo, no install scripts, edits take effect immediately.

**Strengths I'd claim:**

- Comfort with low-level / declarative tooling (NixOS, systemd, flakes).
- Front-to-back ownership of small systems — frontend through hosting.
- A clear instinct for which abstractions earn their keep and which don't.

**Weaknesses I'd own:**

*I don't know when to keep my mouth shut.* I once explicitly asked for feedback from my co-workers at Knotel, and that was one of the bigger things they came back with. We'd had a new manager come in, and because I was contracted in through Tom Digital — a third party — and didn't work for him directly, I just told him whenever he said something I disagreed with. It created tension. I didn't care, because he couldn't fire me: I'd trained his entire team, and what was he going to do, fire the whole Tom Digital company? Knotel relied on us too much by then. The tension was real on their side too; I think they would have liked to get rid of us if they could. They just couldn't. So I let my mouth run against better judgement.

*A chip on my shoulder about being unique.* My current manager has named this one too: everything has to be different, has to be unique, has to be something other than the norm, has to be other than the mainstream. As a self-taught engineer it might partly be a defence mechanism. I don't think that's the whole of it.

It shows up earlier than my career. When I was four I proved to my parents that **Ded Moroz** — Russia's Father-Frost / Santa-Claus equivalent, who turns up at New Year's — wasn't real. I found not only the presents bought for me but also the *receipts*, two weeks before the New Year's exchange. I waited until my parents got home from work and told them: *see, told you. No such thing.* The same year, my very religious grandmother tried to make me religious, and I debunked Jesus too. And the same year, I fell into a fire. Bunch of six-year-old boys plus me at four, no adults around, playing tag around an open fire we'd lit ourselves outside our apartment building — I went down arm-first, my jacket burnt off, second-degree burns down my left arm.

A lot of small things across my life have stacked into something I hold dear about myself but that *is* a problem. I don't know how to fix it. Sometimes I make decisions to my own detriment just to be different.

My wife reinforces this in a way too. She's bisexual, prefers women maybe 95% of the time, and had only a couple of boyfriends in her life before we started dating. We'd known each other and stayed in touch since before my move to the US. Being chosen by her, given all of that, sits in the *I'm something other* column. We're really happy. I'm just naming what it adds to the pattern.

A lot of my friends — obviously joking, they don't mean it — joke that I'm either an alien or a robot. Maybe I encourage it. It started organically in Prague when I arrived here and resurfaced in my closer NYC group too. So something's up. I name it because it's true, not because I've solved it.

*Inconsistency.* There's a classic interview joke:

> *Best quality:* I can knock out a two-week project in a weekend.
> *Worst quality:* I *always* knock out a two-week project in a weekend.

Same trait, but the *always* makes it a vice. I don't consistently learn. If there's a massive challenge in front of me, the likelihood I'll accomplish it is high. If there's no challenge at all, the likelihood I'll just fail is also high. That's probably part of why the startups I've been involved in haven't gone anywhere — early-stage work needs consistency in the absence of immediate payout, and those teams would have been better served by a differently-motivated person.

**Relationship with technology today:**

Torn.

The good: my company pays for Claude Max ×20, and the ease of agentic workflows — fire off a few commands at the start of the day, start seeing results — has reduced an enormous amount of friction. I'm actually working *more* than I did without a fully agentic workflow, not less.

The bad: technology used to be available to anybody who could scrape together enough money for a computer. It's not even much of an investment now to write some JavaScript. But it feels like, to be *competitive*, you increasingly have to pay one of the big AI providers to develop the skills the modern marketplace expects. There are free / open-code models — there are a few decent ones — but they're not as good as the paid frontier models, and the gap matters when you're working against people who can afford the gap. I kind of hate that the industry is drifting toward *only the rich and wealthy can afford to code* as the implicit shape of it. Used to be a good head on your shoulders was enough — if you had a great one, you'd find your way into the big companies and make a good living. The path is narrowing in a way I don't like.

The other half of the same coin: I think we're forgetting how to code. I liked *doing* something myself and seeing the result come up after a few days. That was satisfying — it's part of what made the work feel like work.

In response: my company's stack was stagnating, and my new side projects have started involving **Go**, **gRPC**, maybe a little **Rust**. JavaScript is not the end-all-be-all of programming, no matter how long I've lived in that ecosystem. The Stack Overflow developer surveys show JS in the middle of the pack for compensation per year for experienced developers, and I want to grow. I don't see the next stretch of growth coming from staying inside JS.

### A bigger room

Right now I work in a very small shop — under ten people overall, and that count includes project managers, developers, everyone. I want to land in a *bigger* company next, to see how proper workflows are set up at scale, how proper teams work, what doing this *well* at real size actually looks like — instead of inferring it from blogs and YouTube. If that place runs a mentorship program, I'd happily learn from someone more experienced. I've been the most senior person on too many small teams; that isn't how you keep growing.

### A real itch — embedded systems and the smart-home void

The smart-home ecosystem is *horrible*. There are a few genuinely good companies — **Nanoleaf** is pretty good — but there's a massive lack of smart appliances that **don't spy on you**.

I want home appliances that don't phone home, don't need an internet connection to do their actual job, and let me customise them properly. What's the point of a device calling back to a Chinese / European / American server *for network time*? Why don't you give me a dashboard where I can just point it at my own edge device as the NTP server? That's not a huge thing to ask.

You don't need an internet connection for a smart feeder running on a schedule. You don't need a *microphone* in your smart cat feeder. Why isn't there a proper cat fountain with UV-light bacterial sterilisation and a quiet pump that doesn't clog — one you don't have to disassemble and wash by hand every couple of days?

There's a massive void in that industry. I'd love to get into **embedded systems and Rust** and start my own enterprise in there. It sounds interesting, and the way the world is turning, *locally sourced and manufactured* products feel like they're going to be huge — for political reasons as much as for technical ones. We'll see.
